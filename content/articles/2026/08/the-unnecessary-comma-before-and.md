# The unnecessary comma before, and


## The "AI Slop" Suspicion
A few days ago, I was reading through a piece of text online when a subtle
punctuation habit caught my eye.

"I bought apples, bananas, and oranges."

There it was: a comma sitting right before the word "and" at the end of a list.
My immediate reaction? *Classic AI slop*.

In a world overflowing with ChatGPT-generated essays, LinkedIn posts*, and*
automated emails, we have all developed an internal radar for machine-written
text. To me, that trailing comma felt like an artificial over-engineered piece
of punctuation. Generated by a model trying too hard to be grammatically flawless.

It turns out, I was both completely wrong and surprisingly right.


## The Crowd's Diagnosis
If you have ever looked at a *serial comma* and suspected a robot wrote it,
you are in good company.

As generative AI flooded the internet, readers across the globe - especially
outside the United States - began noticing a sudden, aggressive spike in these
trailing commas.

In British, Australian*, and* international English contexts, people rarely use
a comma before "and" in casual or journalistic writing. When Large Language
Models exploded into public use, readers instantly flagged this specific comma
pattern as a hallmark of machine-generated content.

Linguists and educators have taken notice, too:

- The Academic Red Flag: In studies analyzing AI writing patterns (such as
  research from the University of Cambridge examining AI-assisted student work),
  researchers frequently highlight strict adherence to trailing list commas
  alongside em dashes ($—$) as primary stylistic "fingerprints" of AI text.

- The Classroom Dilemma: On educator forums like Reddit's r/Professors and
  r/UniUK, teachers frequently share anecdotes of flagging student papers for
  suspected AI usage simply because the writing was filled with rigid,
  hyper-consistent *serial commas*.


## Why does AI do this?
Because most major LLMs were trained heavily on American style guides - such as
the Chicago Manual of Style and the APA Publication Manual - which strictly
mandate this comma. Furthermore, AI models are fine-tuned to eliminate ambiguity.

Because an AI applies rules with 100% mechanical consistency, it never forgets
this comma. Humans, on the other hand, are lazy, inconsistent*, and* informal.


## Oxford Comma
So, what is this controversial punctuation mark actually called?

It is known as the Oxford comma (or the serial comma).
Far from being a modern AI hallucination, it dates back to the 19th century.

It earned its famous name because printers and editors at Oxford University
Press made it their official house rule to always insert a comma before the
final coordinating conjunction (`and` or `or`) in a list of `three or more` items.

Without Oxford Comma: "I love my parents, Ram and Sita." (Sounds like my
parents **are** Ram and Sita.)

With Oxford Comma: "I love my parents, Ram, and Sita." (Clearly lists three
distinct entities.)

For decades, the Oxford comma has sparked fierce debates among journalists,
grammarians*, and* authors. The Associated Press (AP) style guide generally
rejects it to save news print space, while academic style guides demand it for
precision.


## "For want of a comma, we have this case."
While debating punctuation on a blog post might seem like pedantic nitpicking,
the absence of an Oxford comma once led to one of the most famous wage disputes
in American legal history.

In 2014, three delivery truck drivers sued Maine's Oakhurst Dairy for years of
unpaid overtime wages. Under Maine state law, workers who worked over 40 hours
were entitled to 1.5 times their normal pay-unless their job duties fell into a
specific list of exempt activities.

See [O'Connor v. Oakhurst Dairy](https://media.ca1.uscourts.gov/pdf.opinions/16-1901P-01A.pdf)

```
The statute (26 M.R.S.A. § 664(3)(F)) stated that overtime did not apply to:

"The canning, processing, preserving, freezing, drying, marketing, storing, packing for shipment or distribution of:

(1) Agricultural produce;

(2) Meat and fish products; and

(3) Perishable foods."
```

Notice the final phrase: `...packing for shipment or distribution of:`

### The $5 Million Debate
The Dairy argued: "Distribution" was its own standalone exempt activity. Since
the truck drivers distributed milk, they were not owed overtime pay.

The Drivers argued: "Packing" was the exempt activity and "for shipment or
distribution" simply described two types of packing. Since drivers only drove
trucks and never packed boxes, they were entitled to overtime pay.

Because there was no Oxford comma between "packing for shipment or distribution",
the sentence was grammatically ambiguous.

In 2017, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the First Circuit ruled in favor of the
drivers. Judge David J. Barron opened the court's 29-page ruling with the
legendary line:

"For want of a comma, we have this case."

Because legal doctrine dictates that ambiguous employment laws must be
interpreted in favor of workers, Oakhurst Dairy settled the lawsuit in 2018 for
$5,000,000.

Shortly after, the Maine Legislature officially amended the law, adding
semicolons to ensure the separation was crystal clear.


## Slop or Safeguard?
The next time you see a trailing comma before "and", do not automatically
dismiss it as AI slop.

While it is true that artificial intelligence has flooded our screens with
hyper-consistent serial commas, that tiny piece of punctuation carries a rich
history - and occasionally, a multi-million-dollar price tag.
